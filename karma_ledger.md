1. Progressive UI Revelation (what you've already started designing)
   The idea that the interface itself is something you "cultivate." Your red square example is perfect — here's how to extend it:
   Early game (Mortal): Stats are raw, unlabeled visual indicators. A red square for strength, a blue circle for agility, maybe a flickering dot for something the player can't even interact with yet (qi, foreshadowing). No numbers, no labels, no stat screen. The player has to feel what things do, like a mortal who doesn't understand the forces governing their life.
   Mid game (Martial Artist → Early Cultivator): You "awaken" and suddenly the shapes get labels. A stat panel appears — not as a UI update, but as a narrative moment. Your character meditates and "perceives their own body" for the first time. The player gets numbers. This reframes every UI unlock as a moment of enlightenment.
   Late game (Higher Realms): The player starts seeing systems behind systems. Maybe they can now see the prestige currency that was always accumulating invisibly. Maybe they realize the flickering dot from hour one was tracking something all along, and it already has a value.
   Games that do this well: Candy Box starts as a text counter and slowly reveals an entire RPG. A Dark Room begins with a single button and unfolds into something enormous. Universal Paperclips progressively reveals that you're building something monstrous. The key lesson from all of them: every UI reveal should feel like a discovery, not a tutorial.
2. Mechanical Metaphor (the game systems ARE the narrative)
   This is where your prestige/Antimatter Dimensions influence gets really interesting with xianxia. In cultivation fiction, "breaking through" to a new realm often means destroying your current foundation to rebuild stronger. That's literally what a prestige mechanic is — you sacrifice progress for a multiplier.
   Ideas to make this diegetic:

Tribulation as prestige. In xianxia, heavenly tribulations test cultivators when they try to ascend. Your prestige reset isn't a menu button — it's a tribulation event. You face it, survive (or don't), and reincarnate stronger. The "are you sure you want to reset?" dialog becomes a dramatic narrative beat.
Cultivation deviation / Qi deviation as failed runs. If you prestige badly or at the wrong time, you don't just lose progress — your character suffers a cultivation deviation. This makes mechanical failure into a story event.
Dao comprehension as automation unlocks. In idle games, you eventually unlock auto-clickers, auto-buyers, etc. Frame these as your character "comprehending a Dao" — understanding a law of reality so deeply that it works for them passively. The player experiences the same thing: they used to click manually, now the game plays itself in that area. They've transcended that level of engagement.

3. Knowledge Asymmetry (what the player knows vs. what the character knows)
   This is where Omori, Undertale, and OneShot really shine — the game knows things about your behavior that your character doesn't, and vice versa.

The game remembers your previous runs, even if the character doesn't (at first). Maybe after several prestiges, an NPC says something slightly different. A mysterious figure recognizes you. In xianxia terms, this could be an ancient being who perceives your "reincarnation cycles." The player realizes: the game knows I've been here before.
Choices that seem narrative are actually mechanical. Early on, you pick a martial arts path — it seems like flavor text. Runs later, you realize it was locking in a cultivation root type that affects everything. The player's ignorance mirrored the character's. This is how Undertale handles its violence/mercy system — you don't realize the mechanical weight of your choices until the game reflects them back at you.
Hidden information that rewards attention. Certain environmental details, NPC lines, or visual cues only make sense after you've unlocked higher perception. You can literally replay early content and see more because your UI now shows what was always there. This is the xianxia "opening the heavenly eye" trope made into a real mechanic.

4. The Player-As-Entity (your god parallel)
   This is your most ambitious and interesting layer. In xianxia, the highest beings manipulate fate, rewrite causality, and exist outside of normal time. The player already does all of these things — they save/load, they prestige, they optimize. The question is: when does the game acknowledge this?
   Approaches:

Late-game NPCs who speak to the player, not the character. Not in a 4th-wall-breaking "I know you're playing a game" way, but in a diegetic "I sense a presence beyond the Dao guiding this one's fate" way. An ancient immortal addresses something they can't see. It just happens to feel like they're addressing you. Stanley Parable does this through the narrator — something in the game world is aware of a guiding force and reacts to it, without ever saying "you are a player."
The prestige layers themselves are planes of existence. Your first run is the mortal world. First prestige opens the cultivation world. Second prestige opens higher heavens. Eventually, the "game" layer itself — the idle mechanics, the numbers, the optimization — IS the highest plane. The player manipulating the meta-game is functionally indistinguishable from a supreme being manipulating reality. You never have to say this. The parallel just exists.
Save file manipulation as a late-game mechanic. OneShot does this — the game world extends into your actual file system. In your case, maybe at the highest cultivation level, you can "edit the Heavenly Ledger" — which is presented as an in-world artifact but functionally lets you tweak game parameters. The player is now editing reality itself, which is what a supreme cultivator does.

5. Emotional/Narrative Diegesis (Katana Zero, Omori style)
   These games use unreliable presentation — what you see on screen isn't objective reality, it's the character's perception. This maps beautifully to xianxia:

Early game is visually and sonically "flat." Muted colors, simple representations. As you cultivate, the game literally becomes richer — more detail, more color, more sound layers. This isn't a graphics upgrade, it's your character perceiving more of reality.
Inner demons as gameplay distortion. During critical breakthroughs, the game's presentation could become unreliable. UI elements lie to you. Stats display wrong. An inner demon trial could mean the game itself is trying to deceive you, and you need your accumulated player knowledge (not character stats) to navigate it.
Memory across resets. Like Omori's handling of repressed memories, certain truths only surface after enough prestige cycles. The character starts having "fragmented memories of past lives" — which are gameplay moments from your previous runs.
